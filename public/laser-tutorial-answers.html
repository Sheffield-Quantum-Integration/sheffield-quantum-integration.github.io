Tutorial 4

Three open-ended questions on laser design

Tutorial Q1 Open-ended

To get high radiative recombination, you need population inversion. Discuss strategies to reduce the threshold current required to achieve population inversion.

Model answer

For an edge-emitting double-heterojunction laser the threshold current density is Jth = q dlas nth / τr, where dlas is the active-region thickness, nth the threshold carrier density and τr the radiative lifetime. A strong answer should attack each term:

  • Make the active region thinner — directly reduces dlas. Use a quantum well or multi-quantum-well design.
  • Improve light confinement — a double heterojunction increases the optical overlap factor Γ, so a smaller carrier density is needed for the same gain.
  • Improve carrier confinement — wide-bandgap cladding stops carriers leaking out of the active region, again lowering nth.
  • Reduce cavity losses — better mirrors and lower internal absorption mean less gain is needed to lase, so nth drops.
  • Improve material quality — fewer defects means less non-radiative recombination, so more of the injected carriers contribute to gain.
  • Manage temperature — Jth ∝ exp(T / T0), so good heat-sinking matters.
Tutorial Q2 Open-ended

List the advantages of multiple-quantum-well (MQW) lasers over bulk lasers.

Model answer

  • Lower threshold current. The 2-D density of states in a thin well is step-like, so optical gain saturates more slowly with carrier density and threshold drops.
  • Narrower emission spectrum. Confined transitions give a sharper gain peak.
  • Wavelength control by design. Both the well width and the alloy composition set the emission wavelength, so the same material system can cover a useful range.
  • Multi-wavelength operation. Combined with the right cavity, the different sub-band transitions in the well can be used to emit at more than one wavelength.
  • Strained quantum wells. Deliberately straining the well lets you grow lattice-mismatched combinations (e.g. InGaN / GaN) and can suppress non-radiative Auger recombination.
Tutorial Q3 Open-ended

Explain why fabricating a room-temperature laser becomes increasingly difficult at infrared wavelengths. Propose how a semiconductor infrared laser can be fabricated.

Why it gets harder at long wavelength

Longer wavelength means smaller bandgap. Smaller bandgap makes Auger recombination dramatically more likely — a band-to-band transition can transfer its energy to a third carrier as kinetic energy instead of producing a photon, and the rate scales like n³ in narrow-gap material. At room temperature, thermal energy kT also becomes comparable to the band offsets, so carriers leak out of the active region. Together these non-radiative loss channels push the threshold up and the efficiency down.

Routes to a working IR laser

  • 1.3 – 1.55 μm telecom lasers: In0.53Ga0.47As active layer on InP cladding, in a double-heterojunction structure.
  • Quantum-well or MQW active region: better confinement of light and carriers, lower threshold.
  • Strained quantum wells: tune wavelength and reduce Auger losses, where crystal growth allows it.
  • Quantum-cascade lasers for the mid-IR: lasing comes from intersubband transitions inside one well rather than across the bandgap, sidestepping Auger completely.
  • Heat-sinking and high-quality epitaxy: reduces thermal and defect-related losses.
  • Active cooling (Peltier or cryogenic) when the application allows it: relaxes Auger and leakage limits.

Quiz 7

Seven multiple-choice questions on laser design

Quiz Q1 Select all correct A, B

A Fabry–Pérot laser has the following design features.

  1. A✓ The refractive indices of the p- and n-layers are lower than the refractive index of the active region.
  2. B✓ The surface from which light is emitted is polished.
  3. C✗ The cavity length is designed so that L = N λ / 3.
  4. D✗ All Fabry–Pérot lasers produce single-mode emission.
  5. E✗ The threshold current is reduced when a thick active region is used.

Why

A. Lower-index cladding makes the active region act as a slab waveguide and traps the optical mode — the higher refractive index of the active layer is exactly what gives the index contrast. B. Polishing (or cleaving along a crystal plane) forms a partially-reflecting mirror — the only feedback mechanism in a Fabry–Pérot cavity. The resonance condition is L = N λ / 2, not λ / 3, which kills option C. Because many integer N can satisfy this for a typical bar length, FP lasers are usually multi-mode (D is wrong). A thicker active region increases dlas in Jth = q dlas nth / τr, so it raises threshold (E is wrong).

Quiz Q2 Select all that are FALSE B, C

Which of the following statements are false for the vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL)?

  1. A· Optical power produced by a single VCSEL is lower than that of a Fabry–Pérot laser. (true)
  2. Bfalse A thick layer of active region is used without increasing the threshold current.
  3. Cfalse Charge injection is more efficient than the Fabry–Pérot laser.
  4. D· The distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) reduces the emission spectral width. (true)
  5. E· At high injection current, lasing efficiency reduces. (true)

Why these two are false

B is false because a VCSEL deliberately uses a very thin active region — its low threshold comes from the short cavity combined with very reflective DBR mirrors (> 90 %). A thicker active region would raise the threshold, not leave it unchanged.

C is false because the thick DBR stacks and the ring-shaped contact (needed to let light out of the top surface) actually make charge injection harder. Current bunches at the edges and the device heats up at high drive.

A, D and E are all genuinely true: a single VCSEL gives less power than an edge-emitter; the DBR's narrow reflectivity peak helps select a single longitudinal mode; and at high drive, Auger recombination and carrier leakage drag efficiency down.

Quiz Q3 Select all correct D, E

A quantum-dot laser offers the following advantages over a bulk laser.

  1. A✗ Better heat dissipation.
  2. B✗ Lower crystal defects.
  3. C✗ Wider emission linewidth.
  4. D✓ Narrower emission linewidth.
  5. E✓ Lower threshold current.

Why

Quantum dots confine carriers in all three spatial directions, producing atom-like discrete energy levels. The density of states becomes a set of narrow peaks instead of a continuous band, so the gain spectrum is sharp (option D) and only a small carrier density is needed to reach threshold (option E). Heat dissipation, crystal-defect density and emission linewidth broadening are not directly improved by going to dots — defect density is set by growth, and bulk lasers can have very narrow linewidth too if they use a DFB cavity.

Quiz Q4 Single answer D

Recommend the combination of narrow- and wide-bandgap materials that you would use to create a double-heterojunction laser emitting at 1550 nm.

  1. A✗ Ge and GaAs.
  2. B✗ In0.53Ga0.47As and GaAs.
  3. C✗ In0.1Ga0.9As and GaAs.
  4. D✓ In0.53Ga0.47As and InP.
  5. E✗ Ge and Si.

Why

1550 nm corresponds to a photon energy Eg = hc / λ ≈ 0.80 eV. In0.53Ga0.47As has a bandgap close to 0.75 eV, so it works as the active layer. InP has a wider bandgap (about 1.35 eV) and — crucially — its lattice constant matches In0.53Ga0.47As exactly at x = 0.53, so it can be grown as cladding and substrate without strain.

The other options fail on either bandgap or lattice match. Ge / Si, Ge / GaAs and the InGaAs / GaAs combinations either emit at the wrong wavelength or are not lattice-matched. Indirect-bandgap materials (Si, Ge) do not lase efficiently at all.

Quiz Q5 Single answer B

Semiconductors such as InAs and InSb do not make efficient lasers at room temperature because…

  1. A✗ they have direct bandgaps.
  2. B✓ their Auger recombination is dominant at room temperature.
  3. C✗ they have high electron mobilities.
  4. D✗ their lattice constants are large.
  5. E✗ they have small electron effective masses.

Why

InAs and InSb are direct-bandgap, narrow-gap semiconductors — good radiative materials in principle. The problem is that their bandgaps are so small (≈ 0.36 eV and 0.17 eV) that an electron–hole pair can recombine non-radiatively by giving its energy to a third carrier (Auger recombination). The Auger rate scales roughly as n³ and grows fast with temperature, so at room temperature it dominates the radiative channel.

A direct bandgap is actually helpful for lasing (rules out A); high mobility and small effective mass are not the limiting factor (C, E); lattice constant has no direct bearing on whether the device lases (D).

Quiz Q6 Select all correct B, D

The refractive indices of the p- and n-cladding layers of a Fabry–Pérot laser with a GaAs active layer can be changed by…

  1. A✗ reducing the width of the Fabry–Pérot laser.
  2. B✓ increasing the doping concentrations in the p- and n-layers.
  3. C✗ increasing the thickness of the p- and n- AlxGa1−xAs layers.
  4. D✓ using p- and n- AlxGa1−xAs layers with higher Al composition.

Why

Refractive index is a material property — to change it you have to change the material itself or the carrier population inside it. D works because adding Al to GaAs widens the bandgap and lowers the refractive index, increasing the index contrast with the active region (this also improves carrier confinement). B works through the plasma effect: heavily doping the cladding adds free carriers whose contribution to the dielectric function lowers the refractive index slightly. A changes the device geometry, not the material, so it doesn't change the index. C changes the layer thickness — that affects the transverse mode profile but not the material refractive index.

Quiz Q7 Select all correct B, C

Select all design options that reduce the emission spectral width of a laser.

  1. A✗ Use a double-heterojunction laser.
  2. B✓ Use a distributed-feedback (DFB) laser.
  3. C✓ Adopt a quantum-well design.
  4. D✗ Incorporate a wider bandgap in the active region.
  5. E✗ Include a metallic reflection layer at the surface of the laser.

Why

Spectral width is set by which wavelengths can simultaneously satisfy the gain condition and the cavity feedback condition. B works because a DFB grating only resonates for one wavelength satisfying the Bragg condition — all other modes are filtered out, giving very narrow linewidth. C works because the discrete sub-band transitions in a quantum well give a narrower gain peak than a bulk semiconductor, so fewer longitudinal modes have enough gain to lase.

A double heterojunction (A) mainly improves carrier confinement and threshold, not linewidth. A wider bandgap in the active region (D) just shifts the wavelength shorter; it doesn't narrow it. A metal reflector (E) is broadband — it cannot select a single wavelength.
